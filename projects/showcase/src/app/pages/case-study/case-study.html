Case study

A design system built from a real hospital floor

Aurora UI started as a problem I lived through: a hospital team losing time on a system that could not keep up with the floor. This page walks through that problem, the architecture that replaced it, and the design decisions it led to.

The problem

Before this project, clinical staff had to open the hospital's ERP just to check a patient's status. The ERP showed the raw data, but reading it took time, and it was not built to highlight what mattered.

Bed status was tracked on a physical whiteboard, updated by hand by the ward clerks, sitting on the wall behind their desks. Updating it took time the clerks did not always have, so the board often fell behind the real state of the ward — and anyone who needed to check a bed had to walk over, or ask.

Abnormal vital signs — a real alarming reading, or just a typo during entry — had no visual priority in the ERP. A critical value could sit in a data table, unnoticed, next to a hundred normal ones.

  • Checking on a patient meant opening the ERP, every time.
  • The whiteboard was manual, so it drifted out of sync with reality.
  • Critical values had no way to stand out from normal ones.

The architecture

I built an integration layer that read directly from the hospital's Oracle database through an API, and served it already cleaned and fast to the dashboard. Every bed, every event, every reading updated on its own — no one had to open the ERP, and no one had to touch a whiteboard again.

Aurora UI, this portfolio version, mirrors that same real-time shape without a real hospital backend: a small RxJS service feeds the Ward 4B demo fake readings the same way a live API fed the original dashboard, so every component you see there — VitalSign, BedCard, OccupancyMeter — updates the way it did in production.

The design decisions

Colour alone was not enough

In the ERP, an abnormal value looked like any other number in the table. StatusPill and VitalSign always pair a colour with an icon shape and a word, so a critical reading is obvious at a glance, not just a slightly different shade in a list.

The board had to update itself

BedCard and the occupancy board remove the manual step entirely: every bed reflects its real state the moment it changes, the way the integration replaced the whiteboard.

Numbers you can act on, not just read

Vitals use tabular numerals so the digits never visually jump while they update live, since someone scanning a wall screen needs to trust what they are reading in under a second.

The result

After the integration, the team had a faster, steadier overview of the ward without opening the ERP constantly. Adoption was not limited to one ward — staff across the hospital started using it.

It also closed a real safety gap: abnormal vitals that used to pass unnoticed in the ERP became visible the moment they happened.
